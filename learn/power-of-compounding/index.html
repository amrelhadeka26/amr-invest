Lesson 6 · The power of compounding

Compounding: let your money make money.

Returns that earn their own returns. It starts slowly, then speeds up, and its most important ingredient is time.

By Amr El Hadeka · 4 min read

If you remember one idea from these lessons, make it this one. Compounding is why small, early, boring investing usually beats big, late, clever investing.

Interest on interest

Put 10,000 pounds somewhere that earns 10% a year. In year one you earn 1,000. In year two you earn 1,100, because now 11,000 is working. In year three, 1,210. The amount you add each year keeps growing, even though you did nothing new.

10,000 → about 174,000 EGP

after 30 years at an assumed 10% a year, without adding a single pound.

For the first years it feels like nothing is happening. That’s normal. Compounding is a snowball: small at the top of the hill, heavy at the bottom.

Starting early beats starting big

Two made-up friends, both stopping at 55, both earning an assumed 10% a year:

Sara: about 1.03 million EGP

500 pounds a month from age 25. She put in 180,000.

Omar: about 797,000 EGP

2,000 pounds a month from age 40. He put in 360,000, twice as much as Sara.

Omar invested more money and still ended up with less. Sara’s secret wasn’t her amount. It was fifteen extra years.

The cost of waiting

Waiting “until I’m ready” has a price. 1,000 pounds a month for 30 years grows to about 2.06 million at 10%. Start five years later and stop at the same age, and it’s about 1.23 million. Those five years cost roughly 830,000 pounds, while you only skipped 60,000 of deposits.

The rule of 72

A quick mental shortcut: divide 72 by a yearly rate to see roughly how many years it takes to double.

  • At 10% a year, money doubles in about 7 years.
  • At 13.9% inflation, prices double, and the buying power of cash halves, in about 5 years.
  • At 30% interest on a credit card balance, what you owe doubles in under 2.5 years.

Compounding also works against you

The same force that grows your investments grows everything that eats them:

  • Inflation compounds every year on cash that sits still.
  • Debt compounds on unpaid balances, usually faster than investments grow.
  • Fees compound too. On 1,000 pounds a month for 20 years, the gap between a 1% and a 2% yearly fee is about 70,000 pounds.

That’s why the order of the earlier lessons matters: clear expensive debt, keep fees low, and invest for a real return above inflation.

Three levers you control

  1. Time: start early, and don’t interrupt it by selling in a panic.
  2. Amount: invest every month, and raise it when your income grows.
  3. Cost: choose low fees. The return isn’t yours to control; the cost is.

The best day to start compounding was years ago. The second best is this month, with whatever amount you have.
